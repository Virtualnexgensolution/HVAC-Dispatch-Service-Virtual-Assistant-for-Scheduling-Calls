What a Virtual Assistant Handles as Your HVAC Dispatch Service
A trained virtual assistant covers the core dispatch workload inside your systems and your rules:

Call intake: Answers or returns calls, captures the customer’s name, service address, equipment issue, and callback number, and logs every contact in your board.
Booking: Schedules appointments into the availability your team defines, applying the correct job type, tags, and arrival windows.
Technician coordination: Relays schedule updates, route information, and customer notes to techs, and flags jobs that are running behind.
After-hours coverage: Captures emergency requests against your written protocol, confirms the on-call rotation, and escalates immediately when the rules say so.
Cancellations and reschedules: Updates the board, offers the next available window from approved rules, and records the change for billing and reporting.

The assistant runs the desk. Your team keeps the judgment:

Diagnosing equipment problems or assessing safety risks.
Setting prices, waiving fees, or approving warranty exceptions.
Choosing which technician handles a complex job.
Deciding whether a situation qualifies as an emergency.
Making retention calls, such as refunds or special discounts.

Virtual Assistant, Dispatch Vendor, or In-House Dispatcher: The Honest Comparison
Each coverage model wins under different conditions, so start with your actual call pattern rather than a promise of round-the-clock availability.

Virtual assistant: Learns your booking rules, works directly in your ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, or Jobber board, and scales hours with the season. Best when you want dispatch coverage that follows your documented process, with hours you can raise during heating season and trim in spring.
Per-call dispatch vendor: Absorbs volume bursts and overnight lines, but agents rotate and may never touch your board. Best when you need raw overflow capacity more than process discipline.
In-house dispatcher: Closest to technicians and customers, but a permanent salary, benefits, and turnover risk attached to a workload that spikes seasonally.

Fix your board discipline before choosing any model: if booking rules, service areas, and job types are not documented, no coverage model will survive an unreliable schedule.

A two-truck contractor gets most calls during business hours with occasional evening requests. Ten hours per week of VA coverage for intake and confirmations may fit better than a full dispatch contract.

A larger team needs overnight emergency capture but wants technician assignments kept in-house. A VA runs daytime booking and after-hours capture while routing judgment calls to the on-call manager.

The Service Standard: Run Your Virtual Assistant Like a Dispatch Service
Vendors sell service-level agreements; with a VA, you write your own. Define these standards in writing before the first live call:

Answer standard: How quickly calls must be answered during covered hours.
Booking accuracy: What a correct booking looks like — right customer, right job type, right window, right tags.
Escalation time: How fast an emergency, safety concern, or out-of-rules request reaches the designated person.
Coverage scope: Which hours and queues the VA owns, and who covers the rest.
Reporting: A daily or weekly summary of calls handled, bookings made, exceptions flagged, and items awaiting your decision.

Then test the process with real cases: a no-heat call, a routine maintenance request, a cancellation, and a caller outside your service area. The VA should record the facts accurately and escalate each case exactly as your protocol requires.

The Pilot That Proves It: Four Weeks, One Scorecard
Structure your own evaluation window and compare results against your current baseline. Keep emergency judgment and technician assignment under your control throughout.

Weeks 1–2: Supervise the VA on non-emergency calls. Review bookings and notes daily.
Week 3: Add after-hours coverage using approved scripts and escalation contacts.
Week 4: Expand to the agreed coverage scope and grade a daily scorecard.

Track answer rate, booking accuracy, escalation correctness, customer complaints, and callback completion. Compare each measure with your own pre-pilot data. Do not treat one strong day as proof that the desk is ready for peak demand.

What Should Never Leave Your Building
Keep authority for emergency priority, technician assignment, pricing and dispatch-fee policy, safety escalation, warranty exceptions, and customer-retention decisions with trained staff.

Document the boundary in writing. Tell the assistant what it may book, what it may record, and who must approve exceptions. For a safety concern, the VA should follow your emergency instructions and contact the designated person, not diagnose the problem.

How to Measure Your Dispatch Desk After Launch
Review performance weekly at first, then set a regular reporting rhythm. Measure answer rate, booking accuracy, duplicate bookings, correct escalations, customer complaints, callback completion, and reschedule handling.

Also compare the monthly invoice with the agreed hours. Investigate any variance by call type, duration, or coverage period, and keep a named internal owner responsible for reviews and process updates.
